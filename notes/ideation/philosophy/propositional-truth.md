# Propositional Truth

## Central Claim

Propositional truth concerns claims that can be affirmed or denied.

A **proposition** is the content expressed by a declarative sentence: what is said rather than the particular words used to say it. The English sentence “snow is white” and a faithful translation in another language may express the same proposition. If the proposition matches the relevant fact, it is true; if it does not, it is false.

This is the clearest home of classical correspondence:

> A proposition is true when things are as the proposition says they are.

Propositional truth is indispensable for logic, science, testimony, law, history, and doctrine because these practices require claims that can be evaluated for accuracy.

## Propositions, Sentences, Beliefs, and Facts

| Term | Role |
|---|---|
| **Sentence** | A linguistic expression in a particular language |
| **Utterance** | A sentence used by a speaker in a particular context |
| **Proposition** | The content asserted or entertained |
| **Belief** | A subject’s attitude of accepting a proposition |
| **Fact or state of affairs** | What makes a true proposition true |

This distinction matters because:

- The same sentence can express different propositions in different contexts: “I am here now.”
- Different sentences can express the same proposition through translation or paraphrase.
- A proposition can be true even when no one believes it.
- A person can sincerely believe a false proposition.
- A true proposition can be uttered deceptively; see [[ideation/philosophy/deceptive-truth]].

## Truth Conditions

To understand a proposition is, at least in part, to understand what would have to be the case for it to be true.

Examples:

- “The door is open” is true if the relevant door is open at the relevant time.
- “Every raven is black” is true if there are no non-black ravens.
- “The meeting begins tomorrow” cannot be evaluated until the speaker, date, meeting, and time zone are fixed.

Truth conditions force vague speech toward explicit structure. They reveal hidden indexicals, ambiguous predicates, quantifiers, and background assumptions.

## Major Philosophical Contributors

| Thinker or school | Contribution | Representative texts |
|---|---|---|
| **Plato** | Distinguished knowledge from opinion and treated rational discourse as answerable to stable intelligible reality. He is a precursor rather than a modern theorist of propositions. | *Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Republic* |
| **Aristotle** | Gave the classical formulation that truth says of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not. He also analyzed predication, contradiction, and future contingents. | *Metaphysics* IV, *Categories*, *On Interpretation* |
| **The Stoics** | Developed the *lekton*, the sayable content expressed by speech, which resembles the modern idea of a proposition. Sayables rather than mere sounds bear truth and falsity. | Fragments preserved by Sextus Empiricus and Diogenes Laërtius |
| **Thomas Aquinas** | Developed the scholastic account of truth as the adequation of intellect and thing while distinguishing divine, intellectual, and linguistic truth. | *De Veritate*; *Summa Theologiae* I, q.16 |
| **Gottlob Frege** | Distinguished the thought expressed by a sentence from its linguistic form and made propositions central to modern logic. | “On Sense and Reference”; “The Thought” |
| **Bertrand Russell** | Analyzed propositions, facts, logical form, and the relation between complex claims and the world. | *The Problems of Philosophy*; “The Philosophy of Logical Atomism” |
| **Ludwig Wittgenstein** | In his early work, treated propositions as pictures of possible states of affairs whose truth depends on whether those states obtain. | *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* |
| **Alfred Tarski** | Formulated a semantic conception of truth using formally specified object-languages and metalanguages. | “The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages” |
| **Donald Davidson** | Used Tarskian truth conditions to explain linguistic meaning and radical interpretation. | “Truth and Meaning”; *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation* |

## Religious and Cultural Traditions

### Classical Greek Dialectic and Stoic Logic

Greek philosophy made assertion, contradiction, and demonstration central to disciplined inquiry. Aristotle’s principle of non-contradiction and Stoic propositional logic helped establish the idea that rational discourse is governed by relations among truth-bearing claims.

### Indian *Pramāṇa* Traditions

Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and Buddhist epistemologists developed detailed accounts of reliable cognition, perception, inference, comparison, and testimony.

- **Nyāya** treated knowledge as veridical cognition produced through reliable means.
- **Dignāga** and **Dharmakīrti** formalized Buddhist theories of perception and inference.
- Indian debate traditions analyzed the relation among a thesis, evidence, example, application, and conclusion.

These traditions do not simply reproduce modern proposition theory, but they show an equally rigorous concern with claims, reasons, and the conditions of successful cognition.

### Islamic *Falsafa* and *Kalām*

Arabic and Persian philosophers inherited and expanded Aristotelian logic.

- **Al-Fārābī** treated logic as a universal discipline for ordering thought and expression.
- **Avicenna** developed sophisticated analyses of propositions, modality, demonstration, and essence.
- Theologians working in *kalām* used formal argument to evaluate claims about divine attributes, causation, freedom, and revelation.

Propositional clarity became essential to determining what a doctrinal statement actually commits its speaker to.

### Jewish and Christian Scholastic Traditions

Medieval Jewish and Christian thinkers used Aristotelian logic to distinguish literal claims, metaphor, analogy, contradiction, and mystery.

- **Maimonides** examined the limits of positive propositions about God and favored disciplined negative theology.
- **Aquinas** analyzed the truth of judgments while arguing that theological language is often analogical rather than univocal.

Creeds, councils, legal judgments, and theological disputes all depended on exact propositional formulation.

### Classical Chinese Disputations

The Mohist Canons investigated names, distinctions, standards, and valid inference. Confucian concern with the **rectification of names** likewise held that social disorder follows when titles and words no longer correspond to conduct and reality.

These traditions connect semantic accuracy with ethical and political order.

## Philosophical Problems

### Truth-Bearer Problem

Are propositions real abstract objects, mental contents, sentence-types, or useful theoretical fictions? If propositions are abstract, how do minds grasp them?

### Truth-Maker Problem

What in reality makes a proposition true? Positive facts are comparatively intuitive, but negative and modal claims are harder:

- What makes “there are no unicorns” true?
- What makes “this event could have happened differently” true?

### Context and Perspective

Many propositions omit conditions required for evaluation. “This apple is red” may depend on perceiver, illumination, visual system, and comparison class. [[ideation/philosophy/relational-truth-qualia-perception|Relational truth]] makes these hidden parameters explicit.

### Future Contingents

Are propositions about undetermined future events already true or false? Aristotle’s sea-battle problem tests whether bivalence applies before an event is settled.

### Semantic Paradox

Self-referential sentences such as “This sentence is false” threaten simple accounts of truth. Tarski’s separation of object-language and metalanguage was designed partly to prevent such paradoxes in formal systems.

## Place in the 5×3 Framework

Propositional truth belongs primarily to **Mental/Correspondence**:

- The proposition is the mental or linguistic map.
- The fact or state of affairs is the territory.
- Truth is successful alignment between them.

But propositional accuracy is not the whole virtue of honesty. A speaker can assert nothing false and still manipulate another person through omission or implication. The ethical mean therefore requires both accurate propositions and a sincere orientation toward shared reality, as developed in [[ideation/philosophy/truth-lies-correspondence]].
