# Relational Truth: Qualia, Perception, and Emotion

## Central Claim

Some truths cannot be described adequately as properties of an object alone. They concern how reality is disclosed through a relation among:

- An object or event
- A perceiving body
- A conscious point of view
- Environmental conditions
- A history of learning and interpretation
- A linguistic and cultural context

Color is the clearest example. The claim “the apple is red” compresses a complex relation among surface reflectance, illumination, visual physiology, contrast, and a learned color vocabulary.

The experience is not unreal because it is relational. A rainbow is observer-dependent but not imaginary. Pain may lack a public shape or location independent of a sufferer, yet the suffering is real.

## Relational Truth Is Not Simple Relativism

Relational truth does not mean:

> Every interpretation is equally true.

It means:

> Some properties and experiences must be indexed to a perceiver, standpoint, or relationship before their truth conditions are complete.

Compare:

- “The apple is intrinsically red under every condition.”
- “The apple normally appears red to human trichromatic observers under daylight.”
- “The apple appears brown to me under this illumination.”

These claims can be assessed. They are not mutually interchangeable, and some may be false. Relationality adds parameters; it does not abolish standards.

## Four Forms of Relational Truth

### Perceptual Relation

How an object appears depends partly on the object and partly on the perceiver and conditions.

Examples include color, sound, taste, temperature, pain, and bodily orientation.

### Phenomenological Relation

First-person experience has a character available differently to the subject than to an outside observer: what it is like to see red, grieve, feel shame, or experience awe.

### Affective Relation

Emotions disclose the world as significant:

- Fear presents something as dangerous.
- Grief presents someone or something as irretrievably lost.
- Shame presents the self as exposed or diminished.
- Love presents another person as uniquely valuable.

Emotions have both phenomenology and possible correctness conditions. Fear can be genuine yet mistaken about danger.

### Intersubjective Relation

Some truths arise between persons rather than inside either person alone:

- A promise exists because speaker and hearer occupy a shared normative relation.
- Betrayal is not merely an inner feeling; it concerns violated trust.
- Social status, money, law, and ritual depend on collective recognition.

These truths are relationally constituted without being arbitrary to each isolated individual.

## Epistemic Authority Without Infallibility

A person generally has special authority concerning their own experience:

- “I am in pain.”
- “That appears green to me.”
- “I feel afraid.”

But first-person authority has limits:

- A person can misidentify an emotion.
- The cause of an experience can be misunderstood.
- Memory can revise or confabulate.
- Cultural vocabulary can make some distinctions easier to notice than others.
- A sincere report can be incomplete.

The strongest principle is:

> The subject is usually the primary witness to the experience, but not necessarily the final interpreter of its causes, meaning, or moral implications.

## Major Philosophical Contributors

| Thinker | Contribution | Representative texts |
|---|---|---|
| **John Locke** | Distinguished primary qualities from secondary qualities such as color, taste, and sound, understood through powers to produce ideas in perceivers. | *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II |
| **George Berkeley** | Argued that supposedly primary qualities are also inseparable from perception, challenging a sharp division between objective and perceiver-dependent qualities. | *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* |
| **Immanuel Kant** | Distinguished things as they may be in themselves from objects as structured by the conditions of possible human experience. | *Critique of Pure Reason* |
| **William James** | Treated immediate experience and relations as philosophically basic in radical empiricism. | *Essays in Radical Empiricism* |
| **Edmund Husserl** | Developed phenomenology as disciplined description of how objects are given to consciousness through intentional acts. | *Logical Investigations*; *Ideas I* |
| **Max Scheler** | Argued that emotions can disclose values rather than functioning only as private reactions. | *Formalism in Ethics and Non-Formal Ethics of Values* |
| **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** | Emphasized embodied perception: the perceiver is not a detached spectator but a bodily participant in a meaningful world. | *Phenomenology of Perception* |
| **Ludwig Wittgenstein** | Challenged the idea of a wholly private language and showed that reports of inner experience depend on public practices of expression and learning. | *Philosophical Investigations* |
| **Thomas Nagel** | Argued that consciousness includes an irreducible point of view—what it is like for the organism. | “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” |
| **Frank Jackson** | Used the knowledge argument to ask whether complete physical information leaves out knowledge of phenomenal character. | “Epiphenomenal Qualia” |
| **Jonathan Cohen** | Defended color relationalism, treating colors as relative to perceivers and viewing conditions rather than as simple intrinsic properties. | *The Red and the Real* |

## Color as a Model Case

Color brings objective and subjective elements together:

- Objects have physical reflectance profiles.
- Light conditions alter the wavelengths reaching the eye.
- Visual systems transform that input.
- Color constancy stabilizes appearance across changing conditions.
- Different species and atypical visual systems partition the stimulus differently.
- Languages vary in how they group and name color regions.

Possible theories include:

- **Physicalism:** colors are physical properties.
- **Dispositionalism:** colors are powers to produce experiences in suitable perceivers.
- **Subjectivism:** colors are qualities of experience.
- **Relationalism:** colors are constituted by relations among objects, perceivers, and conditions.
- **Pluralism:** different concepts of color track different but legitimate aspects of the phenomenon.

The important point for truth theory is that “the apple is red” may be a perfectly useful ordinary proposition while still abbreviating a relational structure.

## Emotion as Perception of Significance

Emotions are not merely internal weather. They are directed toward objects and situations.

An emotion can therefore be evaluated along at least three axes:

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| **Phenomenological truth** | Is this what the person is genuinely experiencing? |
| **Intentional accuracy** | Does the situation possess the significance the emotion presents? |
| **Proportionality** | Is the emotion appropriately intense and enduring? |

A panic response can be phenomenologically real while misrepresenting the present environment as dangerous. Grief can accurately disclose the value of what was lost even when it does not yield a detached proposition.

This connects relational truth to the framework’s spectra: emotions may reveal values while also expressing deficit, mean, or excess.

## Religious and Cultural Traditions

### Buddhist Traditions: Perception, Construction, and Two Truths

Buddhist traditions analyze experience as dependently arisen rather than as a simple encounter between a permanent self and self-existing object.

- Abhidharma analyzes momentary events of cognition.
- Yogācāra examines how conceptual construction shapes experienced objects.
- Dignāga and Dharmakīrti distinguish non-conceptual perception from conceptual inference.
- Theories of conventional and ultimate truth distinguish workable everyday descriptions from deeper analysis of what ultimately exists.

These views do not imply that anything anyone perceives is true. They offer disciplined methods for separating direct appearance, conceptual imposition, error, and liberating insight.

### Jainism: Many-Sided Reality

Jain **anekāntavāda** holds that reality is many-sided and that finite judgments typically express only one aspect. **Syādvāda** qualifies assertions by standpoint and conditions.

The point is not that contradictions are indiscriminately true. It is that unqualified propositions often mistake a partial perspective for an exhaustive account.

This provides a strong cultural analogue for relational truth:

> State the standpoint under which the claim is true.

### Advaita Vedānta: Levels of Reality

Advaita distinguishes ordinary transactional reality (*vyāvahārika*) from ultimate reality (*pāramārthika*). Everyday distinctions remain effective within lived experience even if nondual analysis ultimately revises their status.

This is not identical to perceptual relationalism, but it highlights that a claim’s truth may depend on the level of analysis from which it is made.

### Japanese Philosophy: Nishida’s Pure Experience

**Nishida Kitarō** begins *An Inquiry into the Good* with “pure experience”: the moment of seeing a color or hearing a sound before reflective judgment divides experience into subject and object.

Influenced by Zen Buddhism and William James, Nishida treats immediate experience as more basic than the later claim that an isolated subject is representing an external object.

### Christian Mystical and Apophatic Traditions

Christian contemplatives often distinguish conceptual knowledge about God from participatory or experiential knowledge of God. Apophatic theology emphasizes that ultimate reality exceeds literal description.

This does not make doctrine irrelevant. It claims that some realities are known through transformation, participation, and relation rather than through propositions alone.

### Sufi Traditions

Sufi writers use the metaphor of **taste** (*dhawq*) for knowledge acquired through lived spiritual experience. A description of sweetness differs from tasting sweetness.

Again, experiential access does not automatically guarantee correct interpretation. Traditions develop teachers, practices, and communal standards partly because private experience remains vulnerable to projection and self-deception.

## Dangers and Objections

### Collapse into “My Truth”

First-person reports should not be generalized into claims about external facts without evidence. “I feel unsafe” and “this person threatened me” are related but distinct propositions.

### Incommunicability

If qualia are entirely private, comparison and correction become impossible. Wittgenstein’s challenge is that even inner-language depends on shared forms of life.

### Cultural Overreach

Culture shapes attention, interpretation, and vocabulary, but it does not follow that material reality is produced by vocabulary.

### Emotional Infallibility

Emotions may disclose values, yet they can also be conditioned by trauma, prejudice, misinformation, or misplaced association.

### Erasure of Experience

The opposite error treats only third-person measurement as real. This excludes pain, meaning, beauty, and emotion precisely because they require a subject to whom they appear.

## Place in the 5×3 Framework

Relational truth shows that Correspondence is not always a two-part mirror between sentence and object. It may involve a structured relation:

> Reality ↔ body ↔ consciousness ↔ language ↔ community

It intersects several values:

- **Mental/Correspondence:** Is the interpretation faithful to what appears and what causes the appearance?
- **Mental/Beauty:** What qualitative character is disclosed?
- **Self/Worth:** How is the self experienced and evaluated?
- **Others/Regard:** Is another person’s testimony about experience received seriously?
- **Spirit/Meaning:** What ultimate significance is disclosed through participation?

The virtue is neither detached objectivism nor unchecked subjectivism. It is disciplined fidelity to both the shared world and the situated experience through which that world becomes present.
